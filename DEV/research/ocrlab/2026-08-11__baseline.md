## 5. The extension's largest result: 17 of 45 scenes kill the renderer

The extension edition crashed the browser tab on **17 scenes - 38% of the corpus**. Each crash is
recorded as an errored scene; the runner now starts a fresh browser and continues, so the run
completed with 17 named failures instead of stopping at the seventh scene.

Across the corpus the crashes correlate strongly with image size:

| | Range | Crashed |
|---|---|---|
| scenes at or below 2.8 Mpx | 0.2 - 2.8 Mpx | 2 of 30 |
| scenes above 2.8 Mpx | 3.0 - 17.0 Mpx | 15 of 15 |

The exception in the upper band is `chicken-little-1961-political-cartoon-jpg` at 8.2 Mpx, which
survived. The two below-band crashes are `le-petit-journal-1908-map-labels` and
`cover-of-reggie-no-18-jpg`, both at 2.4 Mpx. Largest crashed:
`le-petit-journal-balkan-crisis-1908-jpg` at 17.0 Mpx. Full list in
`temp/ocrlab/base06-ext/evidence.json`, each with `error: "the DevTools connection errored"` - the
whole browser process dies, not only the renderer, and its stderr says nothing about why.

**A controlled probe refutes "megapixels" as the rule, and that refutation is the useful part.**
Six downscaled copies of `caricature-gillray-plumpudding-jpg` (11.3 Mpx, a dense crosshatched
engraving) were registered as derived scenes and run: **1.0 and 1.5 Mpx survived; 2.0, 2.5, 3.0 and
5.0 Mpx all crashed.** That contradicts the corpus table in both directions - the corpus has scenes
surviving at 2.8 and 8.2 Mpx, and this image dies at 2.0. Two candidate explanations were tested
against the data and neither holds on its own:

- *"The 2x pre-OCR upscale is what blows the budget."* No. The 1.0 Mpx probe estimates below the
  DPI floor and is upscaled to 4.7 Mpx before recognition, and it survives; the 2.0 Mpx probe is
  above the floor, is fed at its native size, and dies. The larger staged image is the safe one.
- *"The rescue ladder's extra full-size renditions are what blows it."* Not supported either. Both
  surviving probes returned a plate, so their first pass read something and the ladder never ran -
  the probe never actually exercised the ladder, so it cannot be what the probe measured. Two
  corpus scenes that *did* run the ladder (`1862-political-cartoon` at 1.9 Mpx and
  `polish-soviet-propaganda-poster-19y` at 1.6 Mpx, both 0 plates) survived it.

What survives both is that cost is **not** a function of pixel count alone. The scenes that die are
dense engravings, halftoned newspaper pages and detailed photographs; the large scenes that live are
flat cartoons and posters. A recognizer's memory scales with the number of connected components it
has to consider, not only with area, which would explain it - but **that is a hypothesis this report
has not tested**, and the probe above is exactly what happens to a plausible mechanism when it is
tested. It is named here so the next cycle starts from a question rather than from a guess.

Peak memory of the lab's own browser, sampled at 700 ms while it died on the 11.3 Mpx scene:
**1 245 MB across 13 processes**, so the true peak is higher than that.

### 5.1 Correction, 2026-08-12: none of this was the extension

The paragraphs above are left standing because they are what the baseline measured and how the next
cycle was aimed; the conclusion they reach is wrong, and the cycle they aimed found out why. Nothing
crashed. The browser survived every one of these scenes - driven for two minutes with the console,
exceptions and target lifecycle subscribed, the page answers, the browser answers, no crash dump is
written and Windows logs no application error. What died was **the lab's own connection**: this
runner asked `Page.captureScreenshot` to render the clip at the source image's natural size, and a
reply past about four megabytes of base64 is dropped and takes the websocket with it. That is why the
failures tracked image size and detail together - both feed the size of a PNG - and why no measure of
the recognizer ever separated them. The connected-components hypothesis named above was tested and
refuted too: counted after Otsu, a surviving halftone probe has 70 200 components and a dying
engraving has 7 083.

So "a reader who opens an ordinary comic-book scan loses the tab" was never true, and the corpus was
never at fault either. The full account, the derived ceiling and the repair are in
[`DEV/plan/2026-08-12_extension-crashes-the-tab-on-a-detailed-scan.md`](../../plan/2026-08-12_extension-crashes-the-tab-on-a-detailed-scan.md).
After it, `npm run ocrlab -- --split all` completes 45 of 45 with nothing errored (`temp/ocrlab/fix02`).

Two consequences for the rest of this report. The extension column of §2 and §4 covered 28 scenes and
now covers 45. The extension's **concealment and contrast** figures are superseded by
`temp/ocrlab/fix02`: both editions now measure a resampled render rather than one measuring a natively
re-rastered one, which moved mean residual ink 0.1170 -> 0.1185 and mean minimum contrast 91.7 -> 82.8
over the 28 scenes common to both runs. Recognition, grouping, position, damage and the geometry gates
are unaffected, and so is `DEV/ocrlab/thresholds.json`, which was derived from the desktop run.

The measurement was only possible because the transport was repaired first - see §7.

## 7. The instrument had to be repaired before it could measure

Recorded here rather than absorbed silently, because the plan's whole argument is that a number is
only worth what the instrument behind it is worth.

**The extension runner could not finish a run.** It hung, permanently, on the seventh scene. The
cause was in the transport, not the browser: `waitFor` in `extension/scripts/_ocrlab-cdp.mjs`
checked its deadline only *after* its predicate returned, and the predicate called
`CDP.send`, which had **no timeout at all**. A renderer that stops answering therefore hung
`Runtime.evaluate` for ever, inside a wait that could not time out, inside a run that produced no
evidence file and no failed scene. Measured while it was hung: 0.4 s of browser CPU per 30 s.

The desktop side never had this problem - `tools/ocrlab/runner/cdp.go` has always bounded each call
at `browserTimeout` (90 s). This was a one-sided gap in a shared contract, and it is exactly the
class of divergence `docs/PARITY.md` exists to catch.

Three repairs, all in the lab, none touching shipped extension code:

1. **`CDP.send` bounds every call** at `CallTimeoutMs = 90_000`, the Go side's value. A call the
   browser never answers now rejects and names the method.
2. **A dead transport stays dead.** The socket's `close`/`error` fails every pending call and marks
   the client, so later calls reject at once. Without this, a crash on scene 7 cost 38 further
   scenes × 90 s of grinding through a browser that was not there.
3. **The runner starts a fresh browser when one dies** and continues with the next scene, so a
   crash costs the scene that caused it rather than every scene after it. The crashed scene keeps
   its recorded error - it is never dropped from the count, which is what §5 depends on.

A fourth, smaller one: the launch now re-reads `DevToolsActivePort` on each attempt instead of once.
A browser starting on a brand-new profile can write a port, exit and be replaced by a second process
on a different port, and connecting to the first then fails with `ECONNREFUSED` for a browser that
is running perfectly. This had already cost one aborted run.

`extension/test/ocrlab-cdp.test.mjs` covers the first three: an unanswered call rejects, a reply
cancels the timer, a socket close fails every waiting call, and the default bound equals the Go
side's. `npm test` 121/121.

## 9. Why step 06.3 cannot yet write most of a `thresholds.json`

Step 06.3 asks for a per-category bound and a non-regression tolerance for each of the eight
dimensions. From this baseline:

- **The four hard gates can be written now.** They are fixed at zero by the strategic spec
  regardless of any baseline - protected-area damage, merges, clipped plates, cross-group overlaps -
  and `Thresholds.Validate` already refuses any other value.
- **A `regressionTolerance` cannot be written at all.** It compares holdout against holdout and
  there is no holdout. Writing one would be writing a field that can never fire.
- **Overall bounds can be written for the dimensions with enough scenes behind them**, each
  carrying the baseline value it came from, and each honest about resting on 10-11 scenes.
- **Per-category bounds cannot be written for rows of one or two scenes** - `poster`, `cartoon`,
  `script` and `document` in both editions. A bound from a single scene is that scene's result with
  a decimal point.

The consequence is deliberate and is recorded in the thresholds file's own `caveat` field: the file
this baseline supports is a small, honest one, not a full eight-by-seven matrix. The corpus, not the
tooling, is what stands between here and the rest of it - **annotate and split**.
